# Client intake triage

Law firms get a lot of email. A firm publishes an address like `info@` and everything
lands there: software vendors selling things, existing clients chasing an invoice,
and every so often someone with a real legal problem who wants to hire them.

Somebody has to read all of it. They work out which is which, pull out the facts that
matter — who is involved, what happened, when, how much money — check whether the
firm is even allowed to take the case, and pass it to the right lawyer.

That middle check is the one that bites. A law firm cannot act against its own
clients. If a new enquiry names the other side of a dispute, and that other side
turns out to be someone the firm already represents, taking the case is a serious
problem: the firm can be thrown off the case, lose the fees, and end up facing a
negligence claim. Lawyers call this a conflict of interest. Checking for it is
routine, dull, and very easy to get wrong on the fortieth email of the day.

This is a working demo of software that does that first pass. It reads each email,
works out what it is, pulls out the facts, checks for conflicts, and then either
routes it to a lawyer or stops and asks a person.

The stopping is the point.

## One email, start to finish

Someone writes in on behalf of their company. They say another company —
Copperfield Dental Associates Incorporated — is selling under a name one letter away
from their registered trademark, and they want it stopped.

The software reads it and works out that this is a new enquiry rather than an
existing client or a sales pitch. It pulls out their name, the company they are
complaining about, the kind of law involved (trademarks), and where they are.

Then it looks up everyone named in the email against the firm's own records. It
finds that the firm already acts for a client called Copperfield Dental Associates
Inc. Same name, spelled slightly differently.

So it stops. It does not open a file, it does not reply, and it does not pick a
lawyer. The email goes into a review queue with the reason written out in plain
English:

> The inquiry names “Copperfield Dental Associates Incorporated” as the opposing
> party. The firm acts for “Copperfield Dental Associates Inc.”, the same name
> differently punctuated or abbreviated — a former client, with no open matters. If
> they are the same entity, taking this matter would put the firm against someone it
> already represents.

Someone opens the queue, reads that, and can see the exact words in the original
email that each fact came from. They decide. Nothing leaves the building until they
click approve.

Twenty-six of the fifty-one emails in the demo end up in that queue.

## Try it

```bash
docker compose up        # then open http://localhost:8000
```

That is the whole setup. No account, no API key, no internet connection needed: the
demo has a recording of every answer the AI gave when it was built, and plays them
back from a file. Same emails, same decisions, every time you run it.

There is more on running it — and on rebuilding after a code change — further down.

## What this is not

Before anything else, four things this demo does not do. It is a portfolio piece,
not a product, and it is worth being blunt about where the edges are.

**None of the data is real.** All 51 emails, 200 clients and 150 matters were
generated by a script from a starting number. No real person wrote any of it, no
real firm's records are anywhere near it, and the names come from word lists. There
is no connection to an actual mailbox either. The inbox is a table in a database.

**The conflict check compares names as text.** It tidies up spelling, expands common
abbreviations, and compares the words. It correctly spots that `Meridian
Manufacturing Corp.` and `Meridian Mfg. Corp.` are probably the same company. What
it cannot do is know that some company is a wholly-owned subsidiary of a client
under a completely different name, because that takes ownership data this does not
have. Real conflict systems are mostly that missing data. This has none of it.

**It knows no law.** Nothing here works out a filing deadline, applies a court rule,
or knows anything specific to one state. When it reads a location out of an email,
the only thing it checks is that the name is a real US state.

**Nothing is ever sent to anyone.** There is no way to send mail from this code at
all — no email server, and no "sent" status for a draft to move into. Approving a
reply marks it approved and that is where it stops, permanently. A test checks that
the only part of the system that can change anything is the review screen.

## Why it is built to stop and ask

Software like this earns its keep by getting through a lot of mail quickly. What
makes it dangerous is that when it gets something wrong, nothing looks wrong.

**A missed conflict does not announce itself.** If the system fails to notice that
the opposing party is an existing client, nothing breaks on screen. A file opens,
work starts, and the problem turns up weeks later as a firm being removed from a
case, writing off its fees, and having an uncomfortable conversation with its
insurer. Now weigh that against the cost of asking: one more email in a queue and
thirty seconds of somebody's attention. Those two costs are nowhere near each other,
which is the whole argument for a system that leans hard towards asking.

So every possible conflict reaches a person, including the weak ones. One of the
rules notices when an email arrives from a company the firm already acts for. That
could be the client's own lawyer writing about new work, or it could be an employee
quietly asking for help with something against their employer. Those two need
opposite handling, and nothing in the email tells them apart. No amount of tuning
fixes that, so the system always asks.

**"We found no conflicts" and "we had nothing to look up" must never look alike.**
Both produce an empty list of problems, and they mean opposite things. The system
records which names it actually searched for, and flags a check that searched for
nothing.

This is not hypothetical. One email in the demo is someone writing on behalf of an
injured friend, without ever naming her. The AI read that correctly and marked the
sender as a bystander rather than the client. But an earlier version of the rules
only checked that *somebody* had been named, so the conflict search ran against the
friend who was writing in, found nothing, and let the email through. A clean result
about the wrong person is worse than no result at all.

**A confidence score nobody can check is worse than no score at all.** AI models are
wrong in exactly the same confident tone they use when they are right, and the
certainty they report about themselves does not mean much. So every fact the system
pulls out comes with a pointer to the words in the email it came from. Hover over a
fact on screen and those words light up.

The trick that makes this trustworthy is small: the model is asked to quote the
email back, word for word, rather than to report where in the text it looked. The
position is then worked out here, by searching the email for that quote. A quote the
model made up simply will not be found, and that failure is visible.

The number a reviewer sees is not the model's own. It is the model's number, reduced
if the quote could not be found and reduced again if the value failed a sanity check
(is this actually a US state, is this actually a date). A fact with no quote behind
it is capped low no matter how certain the model claimed to be. The screen shows
that arithmetic rather than just the result, because `0.28` on its own tells you
nothing, while "the model said 0.95, then could not show me where it got it" tells
you everything.

## The five steps

Each email goes through the same five steps. They are separate pieces of code, so
each one can be tested on its own and it is always clear which step got something
wrong.

| Step | The question it answers | Who answers it |
|---|---|---|
| **Classify** | Is this new work, an existing client, or a sales pitch? | AI |
| **Extract** | Who is involved, what kind of case, where, when, how much? | AI |
| **Resolve** | Do we know any of these people, and is there a conflict? | plain rules |
| **Dispatch** | Which lawyer, and what do we say back? | plain rules |
| **Decide** | Act on this, or hand it to a person? | plain rules |

Only the first two steps involve AI at all. Everything with a consequence — whether
there is a conflict, who gets the case, whether to proceed — is decided by ordinary
rules you can read.

The reply is a fill-in-the-blanks template rather than something the AI writes. A
firm's first message to a potential client is the riskiest thing it sends, which is
why real firms use approved templates for it. The AI reads. The rules decide.

### Where the bar is set

Every "how sure is sure enough" number in the system lives in a single file, and
nothing anywhere else compares confidence to a cutoff. That matters because it means
the caution of the whole system is one short argument in one place, rather than
scattered through the code where nobody can find it.

The numbers were not guessed. The measured confidences clump at the two ends —
either above 0.88 or below 0.60, with very little in the middle — and the cutoffs
sit in that empty gap.

They are also deliberately different from each other, because **a cutoff is a
statement about consequences, not about the AI.** A person's name feeds the conflict
check, so it has to clear 0.70. The type of case only decides which lawyer gets the
email, so 0.60 is enough. Someone mentioned in passing, who no rule ever looks up,
has no bar at all.

### Showing your working

Every fact comes with the words it came from, a confidence, and the sum that
produced that confidence. Every reason an email is sitting in the queue says which
fact or which rule put it there. Every conflict names the rule that fired, the
record it matched, and the exact spelling that matched — because "the system flagged
a conflict" is no use at all to the person who has to clear it.

On screen the email itself is the main thing, not a panel off to one side. Hover a
fact and the words it came from light up in the email. Hover the email and you find
the fact. If the AI ever quotes something that is not in the email, that quote
appears beside it with a line through it, because there is nowhere in the text to
put it. That absence is the finding.

## Measured, not claimed

Because a script invented the emails, it knows every fact it hid in them. That means
the system can be scored against the right answers instead of being described in
adjectives. Running `make eval` against real AI output gives:

```
CLASSIFY   accuracy 98.0% (50/51)
  confidence separation
    mean when correct   0.943
    mean when wrong     0.450     gap +0.493
    mean on ambiguous   0.781     mean on clear-cut 0.958

EXTRACT    practice area 100% (32/32 new-matter)   jurisdiction 100%
           party recall 97.1%   date recall 100%   amount recall 100%
           spans: 98.5% verified, 1.5% no quote offered, 0% ungrounded

DECIDE     agrees with expected action  94.1% (48/51)
           asked when it could have acted    3
           ACTED WHEN IT SHOULD HAVE ASKED   0

RESOLVE    conflict traps caught 7/7
           vacuous checks 7   (nothing to check — not a clearance)
```

Two of those lines matter more than the accuracy percentages.

**The confidence gap** is what the whole design rests on. If the system were not
noticeably less confident when it was wrong, the confidence score would be
decoration and no cutoff could do anything useful with it. The gap is roughly half a
point, and the one email it misread came back at 0.45 — under the 0.75 bar. It got
it wrong and knew it, which is the behaviour you want.

**The two kinds of mistake are counted separately, and one of them is held at
zero.** Asking a person when it could have acted alone costs someone a minute.
Acting alone when it should have asked is the failure this entire system exists to
prevent. Averaging both into a single accuracy score would bury the only one that
matters, so the report keeps them apart and a test fails if the dangerous one is
ever above zero.

### One honest caveat

Across 274 extracted facts, the AI never once quoted something that was not in the
email. So the check that catches invented quotes has never actually had to fire on
this data. It is tested thoroughly in isolation and it works, but calling it
battle-tested here would be overselling it.

## Running it

```bash
make demo                       # everything, on :8000
```

Or, without `make`:

```bash
docker compose up --build       # the same thing, minus the staleness check below
```

It prints what it is holding when it starts:

```
  Client intake triage  ·  http://localhost:8000
  built from 0f55161 (main) · image 9a8ef148270c

  51 emails from seed 20260517, already processed.
  26 waiting in the review queue.

  Synthetic data. Nothing here is ever sent to anyone.
```

### Why the second line is there

The corpus is generated and the inbox is processed *during the image build*, so
every decision — including the wording of every review reason — is baked into the
database inside the image. Nothing is recomputed at startup. That makes two
different kinds of staleness possible, and they look identical from the outside:

| What went wrong | What the banner shows |
| --- | --- |
| Restarted without `--build` | the same **image** fingerprint as before |
| Rebuilt, but the checkout is behind | a new image, an old **commit** |

The fingerprint hashes everything that went into the image, so it settles the
first. It cannot settle the second: twelve hex digits can be compared against the
last twelve you saw and nothing else. The commit can be held against `git log`,
which is why it is there.

The image is built from your working directory, not from the remote, so a stale
checkout produces a stale image no matter how many times you pass `--build`. Only
the host can notice that — it needs a fetch, and the container has no network by
design. `make demo` fetches and says so before it builds:

```
  Your checkout is 1 commit(s) behind origin/claude/inspiring-mendel-mdxt7i.
  The image is built from this directory, so it will not contain them.
  Run 'git pull' first, or build this revision knowingly.
```

`/api/health` reports the same three fields, for when the banner has scrolled away.

If you see `Container ... Running` followed by silence, the container was already
up and compose is attaching to a log stream with nothing new in it — the banner
was printed the first time. `docker compose logs` shows it, and the app is fine.
In a Codespace or a dev container, open port 8000 from the **Ports** panel rather
than expecting a browser to open by itself.

Or locally:

```bash
make setup                      # venv + dependencies
make seed                       # generate the corpus, build the database
make process                    # run the inbox through all four stages
make api                        # API on :8000
make ui                         # front end on :5173, in another shell
```

```bash
make test        # 295 backend tests — no network, no API key, no database needed
make test-ui     # typecheck + 10 front-end tests
make eval        # score the pipeline against ground truth
```

### Reproducibility

Same seed in, byte-identical corpus out: no wall clock, no uuid, no global `random`,
and a fixed epoch. Model responses are cached by prompt hash in
`data/llm_cache.json` and committed, so the pipeline replays offline and produces
the same decisions every time. CI regenerates the corpus and fails if anything moved.

`make cache` repopulates the cache from live calls and needs `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`.
Nothing else does.

## Architecture

```
backend/intake/
  domain/      pure: models, spans, confidence, validators, classify, extract,
               normalize, records, conflicts, resolve, dispatch, policy, review
  llm/         prompts, provider, response cache, offline stub
  pipeline/    orchestrator, runner, batch processing, eval harness
  db/          schema, repository, seeding
  api/         FastAPI — reads the database, calls domain functions, decides nothing
  corpus/      seeded generator, prose naturalisation
frontend/src/  React + TypeScript
```

**Domain modules import no infrastructure.** No FastAPI, no `sqlite3`, no
`anthropic`, no HTTP client. This is enforced, not documented: a test walks the AST
of every module in `domain/` and fails on a forbidden import. That is why every
decision in this system is testable with a fixture string and no network.

**Each stage is split in two.** Building the request and calling the provider is
impure and lives in `llm/` and `pipeline/`. Turning the response into typed data is
a pure function of `(email, response text)` and lives in `domain/`. Only the second
half contains judgement, and it is tested directly.

**Malformed model output is a value, not an exception.** A response that cannot be
parsed becomes a `ParseFailure`, which policy turns into a review reason. A bad
response must not take the other fifty emails down with it.

## Known limitations

Beyond the three headline ones above:

- **Naive matching has a false-positive mode.** Names whose distinguishing words
  match but whose corporate suffixes differ (`… Ltd.` vs `… LLP`) are reported as
  `STEM_ONLY` and graded POSSIBLE rather than treated as the same entity. They may
  be affiliated companies or unrelated ones sharing a name; this cannot tell.
- **Ground truth for incidental conflicts uses the same normalisation as the
  matcher**, so it agrees with the matcher by construction on how names are
  compared. Trap recall, scored against deliberately planted conflicts, is the
  independent measure.
- **Resolve scans linearly.** Fine for 200 clients, wrong for 200,000; the
  token-similarity pass is the part that would not survive the move.
- **Pipeline results are stored as JSON blobs**, so you cannot query inside a run
  from SQL. Adequate at 51 emails, the first thing to normalise at 51,000.
- **No auth, no multi-tenancy, no users.** Review actions are attributed to a single
  hard-coded reviewer so the audit trail has a name in it.
- **The offline stub is not a model.** With no cache and no API key the system falls
  back to keyword heuristics, labelled `stub` in the response, the trace, and the
  eval report. Its numbers measure regexes.
